Part 1What this app is

In plain words

If you are building a house in Hyderabad or Vijayawada, you need cement, steel rods, water pipes and bricks. Prices for these change constantly, every shop quotes differently, and almost nobody quotes the real price — the one that includes tax and delivery to your site.

Build Objects is a price comparison site for exactly those four things. You type what you want, and it shows you what each seller charges delivered to your pincode, tax included, so the numbers can honestly be compared side by side.

The analogy we will use all the way through: imagine a very careful man who walks around every building-material shop in the city each morning with a notebook. He writes down every price. Then he does something unusual — he converts every price into the same form ("rupees per 50 kg bag, delivered to your door, tax included") so they can be compared. And when he is not sure about something, he leaves it blank rather than guessing.

This whole application is that man, his notebook, and the counter he stands behind when you ask him a question.

Two cities. Four categories. Around 1,081 products from 729 sellers, covering 2,005 individual listings.

Under the hood

A local-first price intelligence engine for four construction categories (cement, tmt_steel, water_pipes, bricks_blocks) across two regions (hyderabad, vijayawada).

Next.js 16 / React 19 / TypeScript, with better-sqlite3 running in-process — the database is a file the web server opens directly, not a service it talks to over a socket. Money is integer paise end to end; no floating-point value ever touches a price.

Two processes that never run concurrently: collection (npm run collect) writes, serving (next dev, or Vercel) only reads.

Part 2The three rules it lives by

In plain words

Almost every design decision in this app comes from three promises. They sound obvious. Keeping them is what most of the code is actually for.

Rule 1 — When you ask a question, it never phones anyone.
The answer comes entirely out of the notebook it already has. It does not go off to a website to check. That is why answers arrive in a fraction of a second, and why an answer can never be half-loaded or fail because someone else's website is down.

Rule 2 — A number never appears on its own.
"₹362.90" means nothing by itself. Per what? Including what? Checked when? So every price on screen carries its unit, what is included in it, and how recently it was verified.

Rule 3 — The price you see is the price you would pay.
Plenty of sites show one price in the list and a different one at checkout. Here there is only one price-calculating function in the entire codebase, and everything uses it. There is no separate "display price" that could drift.

Under the hood
Law 1
lib/no-network.ts arms a guard that replaces fetch during a request; any outbound call throws and the URL is recorded. Asserted by the first test in tests/run.ts: an outbound fetch during a request must throw, and the violation must be recorded with its URL.
Law 2
lib/money.ts — formatPaise deliberately will not produce a bare number with no basis attached; the unit is always passed separately. Freshness comes from lib/freshness.ts, which grades an age against a category-specific SLA and returns a state, a dot colour, a human label and an exact timestamp.
Law 3
One resolver — landedFor() in lib/rebuild.ts — serves the results list, the detail sheet and the chat assistant. lib/price.ts states the intent: making it a property of the code rather than a promise in a document.

Part 3The whole system in one picture

In plain words

There are only two halves, and they never run at the same time.

The gathering half runs when you tell it to. It visits the listing sites, reads the prices, cleans them up, and writes them into the notebook. This is the slow, messy, careful part.

The answering half runs whenever someone opens the website. It only ever reads the notebook. It never writes, and never goes online.

Keeping them apart is what makes the site fast and predictable: by the time you type anything, all the hard work has already been done.

Part 4Getting the prices

In plain words

This is the man walking to the shops. In practice the "shops" are listing websites — IndiaMART, ExportersIndia, TradeIndia and a few others — where sellers post what they have and what it costs.

For each site, each product type, and each city, the app downloads the listing pages and reads them. Two things about how it does this are worth understanding:

It keeps the raw pages. Before trying to make sense of anything, it saves exactly what each website sent back. That way, if the reading logic turns out to be wrong, it can be fixed and re-run on the saved copies without bothering those websites again — and anyone can check what the site actually said versus what we recorded.

It writes down its failures. If a site blocks it, or returns nothing useful, that is recorded with the specific reason — "the page had no listings", "the listings had no prices", "nothing was in our city". It never quietly skips something. A silent skip would make the final collection look complete when it wasn't.

No silent caps

Plain: if something stopped us collecting — a site blocked us, or cut us off after 20 pages — it is written down, with an estimate of what we missed. Otherwise the results would look complete when they aren't, which is a lie by omission.

Technical: blocks, rate limits, truncated pagination and login walls are each recorded in source_log with the outcome and the estimated volume missed, and surfaced in the written collection log.

Part 5Cleaning the prices — where most of the honesty lives

In plain words

This is the most important part of the whole application, and the least visible.

Sellers post prices in wildly different ways. One says ₹380 per bag. Another says ₹7,600 per tonne. A third says ₹360 and doesn't say per what. A fourth quotes ₹350 + GST, a fifth quotes ₹413 inclusive.

You cannot compare those five numbers. They aren't the same kind of thing. So before anything is stored, every price is converted into one single form:

rupees per standard unit, tax handled the same way for everyone.
For cement that unit is one 50 kg bag. For steel it's a kilogram. For pipe it's a metre. For bricks it's one piece.

And here is the rule that matters most: if it cannot work out the unit, it throws the listing away. It does not assume "probably per bag". A guessed unit produces a number that looks perfectly reasonable and is completely wrong — a ₹7,600-per-tonne listing recorded as ₹7,600 per bag would sit at the top of every search as the most expensive cement in the city, or at the bottom as an unbelievable bargain, depending which way the mistake went.

Every listing thrown away is counted and reported at the end of the run, under a heading that reads not loaded (recorded, never invented).

The same care applies to specifications. If a listing says "53 grade", that gets recorded as a fact with a note saying the seller stated this. If the app worked it out from something else, it is marked derived. If it is a reasonable industry assumption, it is marked typical. Those three labels travel with the value everywhere it goes.

Under the hood

collector/normalize.ts. The entry point is normalise(raw: RawOffer): Normalised | { ok: false; reason: string } at line 639 — the return type is the design: a refusal is a first-class result carrying its reason, not an exception and not a null.

Unit resolution

normaliseUnit(quoted) at line 37 returns { unit, perCanonical } | null. The null is what makes the refusal possible: no unit, no load. Conversions come from the unit_conversion table, not from constants in code, so bag↔kg↔tonne and m↔ft are data.

Per-category attribute extraction

Four extractors, one per category — cementAttrs (364), tmtAttrs (409), pipeAttrs (467), brickAttrs (544). Each receives a Ladder and a field-recorder fi(field, label, unit), which is how every extracted value gets its provenance row in spec_value.

The provenance ladder

ConfidenceMeaning
quotedThe seller stated it in the listing.
derivedComputed from something the seller stated, with the derivation recorded.

Specs are ordered quoted → derived → typical everywhere they are read, and a field with no provenance cannot render at all.

Other normalisation

  • matchRoster (132) resolves brand names against a per-category roster.
  • canonicalCountry (209) and canonicalMoqUnit (229) collapse free-text variants.
  • moqFromSpecs (322) pulls minimum order quantity out of spec text.
  • buildTitle (829) generates the canonical display title from category, brand and attributes — which is why titles read uniformly ("Bangur OPC 53 grade cement — 50 kg bag") rather than echoing whatever the seller typed. The seller's own listing_title is preserved separately.

Part 6Where everything is kept

In plain words

Everything lives in one single file on disk. Not a database server humming away on another machine — one file, about 30 megabytes, sitting next to the code. The website opens it directly, the way a program opens a document.

That is unusual and it is deliberate. It removes an entire category of problems: no connection to drop, no server to fall over, no network delay between asking and answering.

Inside that file, information is separated into tables — think of them as sheets in a workbook:

  • Products — the things themselves. "Bangur OPC 53 grade cement, 50 kg bag."
  • Offers — one row for every seller selling that thing, at their price.
  • Vendors — who the sellers are and where they are located.
  • Prices — the current best price per product per city, plus the market range.
  • Price history — what it used to cost, so a chart can be drawn.
  • Specs — every specification with its source and how confident we are.
  • …and about a dozen more, for tax rates, pincodes, unit conversions and government rates.

What an "index" is, and why there are two

Finding the word "cement" by reading all 1,081 products every time would be slow — like finding a topic by reading a whole book. So the app keeps an index, exactly like the index at the back of a book: a pre-sorted list of every word and where it appears.

There are two of them, doing different jobs:

The word index handles normal searching, and it knows some words matter more than others. A match in the title counts six times as much as a match in the general description. A match in the brand counts four times as much.

The three-letter index exists only to catch typos. It chops every word into overlapping three-letter pieces — "ultratech" becomes "ult", "ltr", "tra", "rat", "ate"… — so that if you type "ultratec", most of those pieces still match and you still find it.

Part 8Typing — where the suggestions come from

In plain words

You start typing "bang…" and a panel drops down with products, categories and prices. Here is exactly what happens.

First, it waits. It does not react to every letter. It waits 80 milliseconds — less than a tenth of a second — after you stop typing. If you keep typing, the timer restarts. So typing "bangur" quickly asks the database once, not six times.

Why the waiting matters twice over. Without it you'd do six times the work. But there's a subtler problem: replies can come back out of order. If the answer for "bang" arrives after the answer for "bangur", you'd see suggestions for the wrong thing. So every request is given a ticket number, and when a reply arrives, it is only used if its ticket is still the latest one. Older replies are thrown away.

Then it asks three questions at once:

  1. Which products contain what they've typed? — up to 7, cheapest first. That is why the top suggestion is the cheapest match, and why each row shows a real price.
  2. Which categories do those fall into? — up to 4, which become the category chips at the top of the dropdown.
  3. What have other people in this city searched for successfully? — the trending list. Note successfully: a search that found nothing never becomes a suggestion for someone else.

And separately, it shows you what it understood. This is the part most search boxes don't do. Before searching, the app reads your words and tells you how it interpreted them:

  • If you typed ultratec, it says Showing results for UltraTech — search instead for ultratec. It corrects, but it tells you it corrected, and lets you undo it. It never silently changes what you asked for.
  • If you typed a Telugu word or a trade term — సిమెంట్, sariya, basta — it shows you what it read that as.
  • If you typed 4 inch cpvc, it shows a little chip reading nominal bore mm = 25 so you can see the conversion it applied.
That inch conversion is not arithmetic. A 1-inch CPVC pipe is 25 mm. But a 4-inch SWR drainage pipe is 110 mm, not 101.6 mm. Pipe sizes are trade conventions, not measurements. So the app uses a lookup table, and names which table it used — because a plumber who orders the wrong bore has bought a lorry of unusable pipe.

Clicking a suggestion does one of two different things. Clicking a product opens that product's detail page directly — it does not run a search. Clicking a category chip switches you to that category. Pressing Enter on plain text runs a normal search.

Under the hood

Client: components/SearchField.tsx. Server: app/api/suggest/route.ts → suggest() at lib/search.ts:1083. This path never touches the ranker.

The debounce and the sequence guard

React.useEffect(() => {
  if (!value.trim()) { setSug(null); return; }
  const id = ++seq.current;                        // ticket
  const t = setTimeout(async () => {
    const r = await fetch(`/api/suggest?q=…&pincode=…`);
    const j = await r.json();

No spinner, deliberately: the file notes the request lands in single-digit milliseconds off a local file, so a spinner would only ever be a flash. A failed fetch is swallowed — the dropdown is an enhancement; the field still submits.

The three queries

-- 1 · products, cheapest first
SELECT p.product_id, p.title, p.brand, p.category, p.unit_canonical,
       p.image_url, pc.normalised_paise, pc.offer_count
  FROM price_current pc JOIN product p ON p.product_id = pc.product_id
 WHERE pc.region_id = ? AND lower(p.title) LIKE '%typed%'
 ORDER BY pc.normalised_paise ASC LIMIT 7;

-- 2 · category counts (also searches search_blob)
SELECT p.category, COUNT(*) n
  FROM price_current pc JOIN product p ON p.product_id = pc.product_id
 WHERE pc.region_id = ?
   AND (lower(p.title) LIKE '%typed%' OR lower(p.search_blob) LIKE '%typed%')
 GROUP BY p.category ORDER BY n DESC LIMIT 4;

-- 3 · trending
SELECT q, COUNT(*) n FROM search_log
 WHERE region_id = ? AND hits > 0
 GROUP BY q ORDER BY n DESC, MAX(at) DESC LIMIT 6;

Query 1 is a plain LIKE, not the FTS index — cheap and predictable at this scale, and joining price_current is what gives every suggestion a live in-zone price. Query 2 also covers search_blob, so a trade or Telugu term absent from the title still routes to the right category.

The route additionally runs parseQuery(q) and returns category, constraints, matched_vocabulary, correction and unit_bearing — without running a search. Those drive the correction banner, the vocabulary gloss and the "read as" chips. _inch_source names the mapping table used.

Part 9Pressing Enter — the search itself

In plain words

We will follow one real search all the way through: bangur opc 53 grade. That means: the Bangur brand, ordinary portland cement, 53 grade. A specific request.

Step 1 — Reading what you asked for

Before searching, the app breaks your words into requirements rather than treating them as text to match. From "bangur opc 53 grade" it extracts three separate demands: brand must be Bangur, cement type must be OPC, grade must be 53.

Why this distinction is everything. If those words were just text to match against, a Shree Cement OPC 53 bag would score quite well — it matches two words out of three. It would appear in your results. But you didn't ask for "something a bit like Bangur". You asked for Bangur. A requirement is a gate, not a hint.

Step 2 — Finding candidates

Two searches run at once. The word index finds things that genuinely match your words. The three-letter index finds things that nearly match, in case you made a typo. Their results are blended, with the proper word search counted three times as heavily as the typo-rescue one — so a typo can save you, but it can never outvote an exact match.

Step 3 — Applying the requirements

Now each candidate is checked against your three demands. The rule is subtle and worth stating carefully:

A product stays if it contradicts nothing you asked for, and positively satisfies at least one thing.

  • A Bangur OPC 53 bag: satisfies all three. Stays.
  • A Shree OPC 53 bag: satisfies type and grade, but contradicts the brand. Removed.
  • A Bangur bag where the seller never mentioned the grade: satisfies brand and type, contradicts nothing — the grade is simply unknown. It stays, because a seller being lazy about filling in a field is not evidence the product is wrong.

That last case is the important one. Missing information is treated as unknown, not as wrong. The one exception is brand: a listing explicitly recorded as "Unbranded" isn't an unknown brand, it is a stated non-brand, so it is removed from a branded search.

The result: this search went from 85 results to 13 — and all 13 are genuinely Bangur OPC 53. Before this rule was correct, 76 of those 85 were the wrong brand and 14 were a completely different type of cement.

Step 4 — One row per seller

A seller who lists the same cement four times shouldn't fill your screen. So after everything else, results are collapsed to one card per seller, showing their best-scoring listing, with a note saying how many others they have.

Step 5 — If nothing is left

Sometimes a request is too specific and nothing matches. The app does not show an empty page and it does not quietly ignore part of your request. It drops one requirement, tries again, and tells you which one it dropped.

The order is chosen deliberately — least important first. Pack size goes before grade. Grade goes before brand. Brand goes before cement type, because most buyers would sooner accept a different brand's OPC than accept PPC from the brand they named: those are different products under different standards. Bore size is given up last, because a plumber cannot compromise on it at all.

Real example: we stock no GI pipe. Search "gi pipe" and you get pipes, with a clear note saying the pipe-type requirement was relaxed — rather than being quietly handed UPVC as though that were what you asked for.

Under the hood

search() at lib/search.ts:597. The client effect in app/page.tsx is keyed on [query, pincode, sort, category, selections, vendorFilter, colSort, limit], with the same 80 ms debounce and sequence guard, and a spinner only after 150 ms. Page size 24. Each stage stamps a timing that ships in the response.

1 · Parse (timings.parse)

lib/query/parse.ts. Deterministic, in-process, no model call. In order: fractions and inch marks (mapped through a table, recording _inch_source); dimensions, diameters, thicknesses, SDR and pressure class; pack size (which sets unit_bearing); vocabulary including Telugu, Devanagari and transliterations; attribute-bearing terms (opc → cement_type = OPC, cpvc → pipe_system = CPVC) from TERM_CONSTRAINTS; brands, first via BRAND_ALIASES then by exact match against the catalogue's own DISTINCT brand list, two-word form first with a head form so bangur reaches Bangur Rockstrong; then edit-distance-2 correction.

2 · Base rows (timings.fetch)

baseRows(region, category, vendor_id) — one row per offer, carrying only what filtering, faceting and ranking read. Display columns are deliberately absent. Cached on PRAGMA data_version.

3 · Retrieve (timings.retrieve)

SELECT product_id, -bm25(product_fts, 6.0, 4.0, 1.0) AS s   -- title 6, brand 4, blob 1
  FROM product_fts WHERE product_fts MATCH ? ORDER BY s DESC LIMIT 600;

SELECT product_id, -bm25(product_trgm) AS s
  FROM product_trgm WHERE product_trgm MATCH ? ORDER BY s DESC LIMIT 400;

rel = 0.75 × normalise(lexical) + 0.25 × normalise(trigram)

Each set is min–max normalised to [0,1] independently, then fused. Not reciprocal-rank fusion — the file gives the reasoning: normalisation is the accuracy choice and the vocabulary is understood well enough to keep it tuned. Both queries are individually wrapped in try/catch; a malformed MATCH degrades to the other index, never to an error page.

4 · Constrain (timings.filter)

ok = (matched > 0) AND (contradicted === 0)

Both halves are load-bearing. Without contradicted === 0, a constraint is a hint and "bangur opc 53 grade" returns every OPC 53 cement in the region. Without matched > 0, a listing declaring nothing contradicts nothing by vacuum and rides in on lexical relevance alone — that alone took the same query from 85 rows to 139. An undeclared attribute is tolerated; brand is the exception, where absence is a contradiction.

5 · Facets (timings.facets) — see Part 10

Part 10The filters on the left

In plain words

The "Narrow by" column shows checkboxes with numbers beside them: OPC 117, PPC 94. That number is a promise: tick this box and you will be looking at that many sellers. Not approximately. Exactly.

That sounds trivial and it isn't. The count and the list have to be counting the same kind of thing. If the filter counts listings while the page shows one card per seller, the numbers never agree and every count is a small lie. Both now count sellers.

Counting while you already have filters on. Suppose you've ticked "OPC". What number should "PPC" show? If it counted only within your current results, it would show 0 — you've filtered PPC out. Useless. So each filter's counts are worked out ignoring its own group's selections but respecting everyone else's. That way you can always see what switching to PPC would give you.

Options with nothing behind them are crossed out, not hidden. If no seller has white cement, "White cement" stays visible with a line through it. An option that silently disappears teaches you nothing; one crossed out tells you the catalogue doesn't go there.

The filters shown depend on what you're looking at — pipes get bore size and pressure class, cement gets type and grade. And the rail only appears once the app is confident which category you're in; showing the cement filters over a mixed list would be a promise it couldn't keep.

Under the hood

Definitions live in the facet_definition table — label, control, cluster, visibility, dependency, sort order, and the why string shown to the buyer — so the rail is data, not code.

The rail renders only when facetCategory resolves: chosen explicitly, inferred by the grammar, or because one category holds ≥ 60% of the result set.

Cross-selection counting, in one pass

For each row, one pass computes the list of selection-bearing facets it fails. A row is in facet f's base exactly when that list is empty, or contains only f. This replaces a per-facet others.every() rescan and answers the filter stage at the same time.

Because a vendor can carry both OPC and PPC, per-value counts legitimately overlap and their sum can exceed the total — each individual count remains a truthful answer to "how many sellers will I see". A test in tests/run.ts clicks through every facet value and asserts search(...with that selection).total === value.count.

Facet predicates are compiled once per query rather than per row: each label's range, under/over and lead-time patterns are parsed out of the inner loop, which took the naive O(facets × values × rows) shape down to something that runs in single-digit milliseconds. __facetEquivalence() exists to assert the fast path and the readable definition agree.

Part 11Why that one is first

In plain words

Once the app knows which sellers qualify, it has to decide what order to show them in. It scores each one out of 1 on eight things, adds the scores with different weights, then subtracts penalties.

What it looks atWeight
How closely it matches what you typed31%
Whether it matches the exact specification you asked for16%
How cheap it is, delivered, compared with the others16%
Whether it is in stock and can reach you10%
Seller rating and reliability10%
Whether it carries a BIS certification7%
How fast it can be delivered6%
How recently we verified the price4%

Then penalties: a stale price loses 0.25, out of stock loses 0.60, and a minimum order bigger than the quantity you want loses 0.35.

Notice price is only 16%. This is a price comparison site, and price is not the biggest factor. That is deliberate: the cheapest listing is often stale, out of stock, or from a seller who can't deliver. If you want pure price, the "cheapest" sort is one click away — and then it really is pure price.

Every card tells you why it is there. A small line at the bottom of each card names the two things that contributed most to its position — "closest match to your search · matches the exact spec you asked for". This is not decoration. Indian e-commerce rules require the main ranking factors to be disclosed in plain language, and that line is generated from the same function that does the ranking, so it cannot drift out of date.

Related: there is a ninth factor, "most bought", weighted at 5%. There is no order history in this build, so it would be a constant — the same for everything, therefore meaningless. Rather than let a dead factor absorb 5%, its weight is redistributed to matching and price, and the factor reports zero. It is not faked with a plausible-looking number.

Part 13How one price is built

In plain words

A seller says "₹350". Here is everything that happens before that becomes the ₹362.90 you see.

  1. Work out the price before tax. Some sellers quote with GST included, some without. Both are converted to the same "before tax" figure, otherwise the comparison is meaningless from the start.
  2. Add GST back, at the correct rate. The rate comes from the product's HSN tax code, not from a guess.
  3. Add delivery. Calculated from the straight-line distance between the seller's location and your pincode, the weight and bulk of the goods, and how much you are buying — because a lorry-load spreads its delivery cost over more bags than a single bag does. If the seller offers free delivery above a certain order value, that is applied.
  4. Add handling and loading. Real costs at both ends that quotes usually omit.
  5. Divide down to one standard unit. Rupees per bag, per kilo, per metre, per piece.
That last figure is the only one ever used for comparison. Every sort, every "cheapest", every filter band works on it and nothing else. That is what makes two sellers who quote in completely different ways genuinely comparable.

One more detail that matters: all money is stored as whole numbers of paise, never as decimal rupees. Decimals drift — add ₹0.10 ten times in a computer and you may not get exactly ₹1.00. Whole paise cannot drift.

Part 14The chat assistant

In plain words

The panel on the right answers questions in ordinary language — what is cement going for today?, how much cement for a 20 × 15 foot slab? — and it can do arithmetic a buyer actually needs, like how many bricks a wall takes.

It uses an AI model, and the interesting part is how tightly it is fenced in.

The design in one line: the AI is allowed to write sentences, but it is not allowed to know anything. Every fact must come from a database lookup performed during that exact conversation turn.

Here is how that is enforced, and it is unusual. Most systems ask the AI to cite its sources and trust it. This one does the opposite: it checks the AI's homework mechanically.

  1. Every time a lookup runs, it deposits every number and name it stands behind into a list.
  2. When the AI writes its reply, every number and every name in that reply is checked against the list.
  3. Anything not on the list is rejected. The AI is shown exactly what it got wrong and gets one chance to rewrite.
  4. If it fails again, the app throws the sentence away and shows you the raw data instead, saying so.

So an invented price cannot reach you. Not because the AI is well behaved, but because a number that isn't in the ledger simply cannot get through.

Two other guards: questions completely outside construction materials are refused by a plain pattern check before the AI is ever called, which costs nothing; and repeated questions are answered from a cache, which is cleared automatically whenever new prices are collected, so a cached answer can never be stale.

Under the hood

lib/chat/ over Gemini 2.5 Flash via hand-rolled fetch (no SDK). Route at app/api/chat/route.ts, streaming SSE, with an in-memory token bucket (capacity 8, refill 0.5/s) returning 429 + Retry-After.

1

Deterministic layers in lib/chat/validator.ts: unsafe, injection patterns, non-Latin bypass, then domain-signal absence (only fires at ≥ 4 words).
2
Answer cache
sha1(data_version + pincode + normalise(message)). First turns only, model-spending turns only, never a failure, never a turn carrying a grounding violation. LRU 400.
3
Tool loop — up to 4 rounds
Eight tools in lib/chat/tools.ts: catalogue scope, product search, product detail, quality ranking, quantity estimation, price estimation, option lists, comparison. Each returns a payload and a FactLedger.
4
Grounding validation
Numbers are checked with a magnitude-scaled tolerance; capitalised runs are checked as entities, with exemptions for user-supplied values, a closed domain vocabulary and sentence-initial words. A toolless reply containing a rupee sign or a superlative is itself a violation.
5

The repair call carries the violations and no tool schemas. If it errors or fails revalidation, the turn returns honestFallback() with refused: true, keeping the UI blocks and dropping the prose.

A draft that is only too long is trimmed locally rather than re-asked — it already cleared the grounding checks, so a sentence-boundary prefix of it is grounded too, and truncation can only drop, never invent. A parity test asserts that every tool's own payload passes its own validator: if a tool cannot survive a verbatim quote of itself, the model can never win.

Quantity estimation is fully deterministic — lib/chat/estimator.ts and coefficients.ts, cited to IS codes, returning the arithmetic, every coefficient used and every assumption. The model never multiplies anything.

Part 15Why it is fast

In plain words

Target: a fifth of a second from your keystroke to the results on screen — including the 80 ms it deliberately spends waiting for you to stop typing. Four things get it there.

It never goes online. Reading a local file takes microseconds; a network round trip takes tens of milliseconds at best.

It remembers the candidate list. The set of cement offers in Hyderabad doesn't change between your keystrokes, so it is read once and reused.

The clever bit is knowing when to forget. A cache that hangs on to old data is worse than no cache. SQLite keeps an internal counter that ticks up whenever a different program writes to the file. The app checks that counter; if it moved, the collector has run and everything cached is dropped. No timers, no guessing, no window in which stale prices are served.

It only fetches what it will show. Ranking works on a slim version of each row. Titles, seller names and tax codes are fetched only for the 24 rows that will actually appear.

It avoids repeated work. Each product's details are decoded once per request rather than once per filter — that alone was about 8,000 redundant operations on a single cement search.

Under the hood
base-row cache
baseCache keyed on (region, category, vendor), guarded by PRAGMA data_version. SQLite bumps it whenever another connection commits, so a collection run in a separate process invalidates it with no signalling. A wall-clock TTL would serve a stale catalogue for its duration; an in-process counter would never see the collector.
same-connection blind spot
data_version does not move for commits on the same connection, so rebuildPrices() calls invalidateSearchCache() directly — correctness does not depend on the collector happening to be a separate process.

Part 16How we know it is true

In plain words

There are 436 automated checks. They run in a few seconds without an internet connection, and they mostly do not test that the code runs — they test that its promises hold.

  • Trying to go online during a request must fail.
  • The quantity calculations are checked against published engineering figures.
  • Every filter count is clicked through and must equal the number of results it produced.
  • A brand search must not return another brand.
  • No product card may carry a photograph naming a different manufacturer.
  • Every colour pairing in the interface must pass the standard contrast requirement.
The test the whole assistant rests on. Each of the eight database lookups is asked to produce its own output, and then that output is fed through the AI's own fact-checker. If a lookup hands the AI a number the checker would reject, the AI is being set up to fail through no fault of its own. That is exactly what was happening: the assistant was rejecting its own database — which is why "which is the best cement?" used to fail every single time.

And a category of test that is easy to forget: checking the guard doesn't fire on correct answers. A safety check that rejects true statements gets switched off within a week — and then it isn't protecting anything at all.

Part 17How it goes live

In plain words

The site runs on Vercel. Since the whole database is a file, deploying it means shipping that file along with the code.

There's a wrinkle: the working database has a companion "journal" file of recent changes, which can't be shipped safely. So before deploying, a command folds the journal into a single clean file — a snapshot — and that snapshot is what goes live. In production the app opens it strictly read-only, so nothing can accidentally write to it.

This has one consequence worth stating plainly: the live site's prices are frozen at the moment the snapshot was taken. Collecting new prices locally does not update the live site. Someone has to take a new snapshot and deploy. Forget that step and the site quietly serves old prices — which happened here: the deployed snapshot was four days behind.

Under the hood
  1. npm run collect — writes data/buildobjects.db.
  2. npm run db:snapshot — scripts/db-snapshot.mjs folds the WAL and writes data/buildobjects.prod.db, reporting size, integrity and row counts.
  3. npm test — 436 tests must pass.
  4. npm run build — Next production build.
  5. git push origin main — the Vercel GitHub integration builds and promotes to production.

.gitignore excludes the working store and its WAL/SHM but not data/buildobjects.prod.db. .vercelignore re-excludes them for CLI deploys, since the CLI does not apply .gitignore to a linked project, and additionally drops collector/raw, collector/fixtures, data/snapshots and tests — nothing on the query path reads them.

lib/db.ts:43 opens with { readonly: true, fileMustExist: true } when READ_ONLY. logSearch's try/catch is what makes that safe: search logging fails silently rather than 500-ing the request.

Secrets are environment variables, never committed — .env* is gitignored, and GEMINI_API_KEY is set in the Vercel project rather than in the repository.

Part 19What is still missing

In plain words

Stated here rather than left to be discovered.

  • "Trending here" never appears in the search dropdown. Recent searches do work.
  • "Most bought" is not real. There is no order history, so the factor is reported as zero and its weight given to other factors — rather than faked.
  • No GI pipe in stock. Searching for it relaxes the requirement and says so.
  • Five product cards in five hundred show no photograph, because their only photo named a different product. That is the intended trade — no picture beats a wrong picture.
  • The assistant's cache and rate limit are per-instance on Vercel, so they reset on a cold start. Answers are unaffected; only how often a repeat question is free.
  • The government cement rate is a scanned PDF with no text layer. The figure is not machine-extracted, and the interface says so rather than printing a number nobody verified.
  • Sand and aggregate have quantities but no prices. The assistant will compute how much you need and tell you plainly that it cannot price it.
